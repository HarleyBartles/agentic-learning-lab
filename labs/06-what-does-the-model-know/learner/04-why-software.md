# Exercise 4 — Why software?

You have seen that a closed-book model is not empty, that its retained knowledge has uneven boundaries, and that retrieval can materially improve the grounds for an answer.

Now turn the question around.

## 1. Does it bring *deep* knowledge anywhere?

Without asking it to retrieve anything new, discuss:

> Does this model seem to bring genuinely deep useful knowledge in any domains before we deliberately provision specialist material?

Name a few candidates.

Then focus on software engineering.

## 2. Probe the breadth conversationally

Ask the model about several software-engineering areas, not just one coding problem.

For example:

- What is the difference between unit, integration, and end-to-end testing?
- How would you reason about an API boundary?
- What makes a database schema brittle or maintainable?
- What would you inspect when debugging an intermittent failure?
- What trade-offs can exist between a simple implementation and a scalable one?
- What is source control buying us beyond backup?

You do not need to become a programmer or judge every technical detail.

Look at the *shape* of the answers:

- vocabulary;
- breadth;
- ability to connect concepts;
- awareness of trade-offs;
- apparent depth without us supplying a software handbook.

## 3. Ask why this might be different

Discuss:

> Why might software engineering be a special case for a coding-capable model?

Possible ideas are welcome, but keep observation separate from things we cannot directly prove about hidden training history.

The useful observation is simply that current coding-capable models can appear unusually well equipped here before we deliberately provision project-specific expertise.

## 4. End on the unresolved question

Now ask:

> If the model already brings unusually strong software-engineering knowledge, what can a real software-engineering domain expert still add?

Do not solve it yet.

That is the next lab.

You are finished when that question feels worth answering.
